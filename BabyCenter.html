Why are my breastfed baby's poos often green?

Medically reviewed by Jane Barry, RN, Child Health Nurse
Written by Sabrina Rogers-Anderson | 21 Jul 2023
Photo credit: iStock
Listen to audio version of this article

Instaread
Before you became a parent, it’s likely you’d never have thought you’d have so many questions about your baby’s poo. From how often they poo, to the colour and texture, working out what’s normal can be tricky. It’s even harder if it’s a strange colour like green! The good news is that green poo in a healthy and thriving breastfed baby is often normal and not cause for concern.

What does green poop mean?

Here are some of the reasons your baby might have green poop:

  • Meconium: Your baby’s first poo (meconium) will be thick, sticky and blackish green. Dark green poos can last for a few days after birth. After that they tend to turn a yellow or mustard colour. This change in colour happens because the baby is digesting milk.
  • Infrequent green poos: Breastfed babies’ poo can vary in colour and texture. One or two green poos, every so often, is nothing to worry about.
  • Too much lactose: If your baby’s poos are green and frothy, they may have lactose (milk sugar) overload. This happens when babies take large amounts of breast milk and their gut cannot digest so much lactose at once. Common causes include an oversupply of milk (usually in the first few weeks after birth) or swapping to the second breast too quickly. Because they’re taking in a large amount of milk from both breasts, it’s passing through their system before the lactose can be broken down.
  • Diarrhoea: While this is rare in breastfed babies, frequent green, smelly poo that explodes or seeps out of your baby’s nappy could suggest an infection.
    (ABA 2022, ABA 2022-2, Pregnancy, Birth & Baby 2023, Raising Children 2020, Raising Children 2021, Raising Children 2022)

What should I do if my baby’s poop is green?

If you’re concerned about the colour, texture or smell of your baby’s poo, take them to your GP or a paediatrician, to rule out illness.

How can I combat lactose overload?

If you suspect lactose overload, there are a few things you can try to correct it:

  • Try lying back or on your side while breastfeeding to give your baby more control over your milk flow.
  • Try block feeding. This can help to slow the rate of milk flow by feeding on one breast each feed. Set a 4 -hour time period and offer the same breast each time your baby wants to feed. Offer the other breast for the next 4 hours. (ABA 2022-2)
  • As well as feeding from one breast each feed, try spacing feeds at least two and a half hours apart.
  • Make sure your baby is attached well to your breast. (A midwife or lactation consultant can help if you’re unsure.)
    (ABA 2023, LLLI n.d., Raising Children 2022)
If you still have questions or concerns, speak to a healthcare professional.
